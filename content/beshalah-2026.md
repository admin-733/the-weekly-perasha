Throughout their sojourn in the desert, the Jewish People were sustained by three miracles in the merit of their leaders: the manna through Moshe, the well through Miriam, and the clouds of glory through Aharon (Taanit 9a).

Our perasha details the story of the manna (Shemot 16:4-36): Having just witnessed the splitting of the sea, the Jewish People began to complain about the lack of food and water. They even suggested it would have been better to die as slaves than to starve in the desert.

Upon hearing these complaints, G-d immediately provided the manna, just as He had provided water at Marah. The people were saved; they now had food and water daily. Yet it almost feels as if G-d was waiting for them to complain. Why the delay if He seemingly had the sustenance ready all along?

The Zera Shimshon is bothered by a similar irony at the end of the shirah. The pasuk states that if we listen to G-d and keep His laws, He will never afflict us with sickness, yet concludes by calling G-d our "Healer" (Shemot 15:26). If we are never sick, why do we need a Healer?

The Zera Shimshon concludes that the sickness itself, when it comes, transforms us. Through the struggle, we reach a higher spiritual place. The experience of being vulnerable, turning to G-d, *is* the healing.

We can apply this to the manna. Just as sickness becomes spiritual healing through struggle, hunger became trust through waiting. G-d chose not to provide a permanent stockpile. Instead, He supplied it daily, and only after the people realized they had nothing of their own, in order to foster a constant realization of man's total dependence on G-d.

The Ramban states this was the true test of the manna. Could the people trust that tomorrow's bread would come? Those who lacked faith were plagued by anxiety and hoarded leftovers despite Moshe's warning. The manna wasn't just about food; it was a daily exercise in bitachon, in recognizing that G-d is the ultimate Source.

Some have the custom to recite the passage of the manna daily as a segulah for earning a living. The passage doesn't promise wealth; it teaches a deeper truth: everything comes from G-d. The doctors who provide healing, the police officer who wrote you that ticket, every paycheck you receive, it all comes from Him.

The more we recognize and openly state, 'G-d, everything is from You,' the more we merit to see His hand working positively in our lives. Every day is another chance to build bitachon, to trust that G-d will provide what we need, exactly when we need it. The more we focus on this fact, the more success we will see in our lives, guaranteed.
