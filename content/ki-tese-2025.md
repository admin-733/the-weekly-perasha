title: "The Lesson of the Rebellious and Wayward Son"
parsha: "Ki Tese"
year: 2025

---

Towards the end of last week's perasha, Shofetim, the Torah stated many halachot regarding when the Jewish People go out to war, who is eligible, what must be done, and how it must be carried out.

In this week's perasha, Ki Tese, the Torah shifts from the physical battlefield to the spiritual front, guarding the neshamot of the soldiers. Back then, the women of the enemy would dress in seductive clothing to entice their captors. The Torah says that if a soldier sees a beautiful woman among the captives and desires her, he cannot simply act in the moment. Instead, he must go through an elaborate and lengthy process. Only after that time can he decide if he truly wants to marry her.

The Torah then continues with the case of a man who has two wives, one loved and one hated. Even in such a situation, the firstborn son of the hated wife must still be given his double portion. Rashi states that these cases are not random. The Torah is building a sequence. The next case, of the rebellious and wayward son, he is the child of that very hated wife, who was originally the captive woman.

With that in mind, let us now focus on the case of the rebellious and wayward son. There are pesukim in the Torah that, without question, stir an emotional response for me, and this is one of them. The idea that a father and mother would knowingly bring their own son before the elders, knowing that if he is found guilty, he could be stoned and hung, always leaves me with mixed emotions. Even the worst parents, even those who neglect their children, I don't believe would knowingly and publicly bring their son to be potentially convicted. The Torah wants us to feel that tension, to wrestle with it, and to ask why it was written this way. I truly believe that this emotional discomfort is itself part of the lesson.

The Gemara in Sanhedrin (67a-72a) goes to great lengths to define the requirements needed in order for a wayward and rebellious son to actually be found guilty and put to death. The conditions are so specific and so narrow that the case is virtually impossible to carry out. It is then with much relief that Ribbe Yehudah states that there never was such a case and there never will be. Ribbe Yose on the other hand states that while it's rare, it is possible (71a).

Consider just a few of the requirements: The son must be a certain age, approximately 13 (68b-69a) and he must eat and drink a very particular amount of meat and wine (70a). The focus on meat and wine underscores that the son is squandering family resources on luxury and excess, which is part of why the Torah sees him as a sign of future destructive behavior. In addition, he must steal the food from his father and consume it outside his father's property. If he eats it at home, or steals from others instead, the law does not apply (69b-70a).

Another condition concerns how the case is brought before the elders. For example, both parents must agree to bring him before the court. If only one parent wishes to prosecute, the case is dismissed; this is because the Torah states "he will not obey our voice," implying a unified parental voice (71a). In fact there are many more stipulations regarding the parents.

All of this leads us to ask: if this case never happened and perhaps never could, why would the Torah devote any pesukim to it? After all, we know that not a single letter, not even a vowel, is extra. Everything in the Torah is exact, chosen by G-d. Even the "stories" we know so well, like those of the Abot, are not just stories for reading. They are there to teach us lessons for life. So why here? Why dedicate space in the Torah to a case that never happened and maybe never will?

Several important reasons are given by the sages and commentators. One explanation is the moral and educational purpose of the law. Ibn Ezra writes (Debarim 21:18) that the Torah presents the rebellious and wayward son as a warning about the dangers of extreme rebellion and disrespect toward parents and authority. The son's behavior represents a rejection not only of his parents, but also of Divine authority itself. His gluttony and drunkenness highlight the dangers of indulgence and excess, showing how such behavior can quickly spiral into a broader moral and spiritual collapse. Ramban states that this case of the rebellious and wayward son is a direct contradiction to the commandment given earlier of "you shall be holy" (Vayikra 19:2) and thus describes how the rebellious son's conduct directly contradicts the pursuit of holiness. The Gemara states these laws were given merely to expound them and receive reward teaching us their primary role is moral, educational, and preventative. (71a)

A second explanation is the preventive and reflective lesson the Torah wants to teach us. The Torah reminds us of the harsh potential punishment, dramatizing the consequences of unchecked rebellion at a young age. This serves as a wake up call for parents and society to take education, discipline, and guidance seriously before it is too late (71a).

A third explanation is the demonstration of Divine justice. The rebellious son is punished according to what his end will be. The Torah looks at his trajectory and the destruction it will inevitably bring if left unchecked. Just as the Torah shows the trajectory of circumstances from marrying the captive woman to now dealing with her child, this is one of the rare times the Torah speaks of judging based on where a person is heading, teaching us the gravity of choices and their long term consequences (72a-71ba).

Finally, it can be explained that the law has a symbolic role. The rebellious and wayward son represents tensions that can exist within the family and within society. It is a warning of how alienation, lack of respect, and rebellion can destroy not only individuals, but entire communities if left unaddressed.

In the end, the Torah is not just telling us about a rare case that may never have happened, but teaching timeless lessons: rebellion and indulgence are dangerous, discipline must be taken seriously, our choices have consequences, and respect and gratitude hold families and communities together. These ideas may not grab headlines but they are the pillars of our families, our communities, and our service to G-d.

As parents, teachers, and even siblings, we can each take small steps to see every child through his or her own perspective, giving them the love, patience, and tools to grow. The care we invest today is not only for that child, but for the generations that will follow, ensuring the Torah's warning of the rebellious son remains only a lesson, never a reality.
