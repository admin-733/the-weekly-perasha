At the start of Perashat Shelah, Moshe sends twelve spies to scout the land, and the people eagerly await their report (Bemidbar 13:1-3). However, the anticipation quickly turns to disaster when ten of the twelve return with a bad report, claiming the land is overrun by giants and other enemies they cannot possibly conquer, in their view. At this point they declare that they "felt like grasshoppers," meaning that they didn't just fear the enemy, they completely lost their self-worth, leaving them feeling insignificant, powerless, and defeated before the battle even began (Bemidbar 13:31-33).

Without a second thought, the people accept the report, crying and bemoaning their fate. They even feel defeat before ever even stepping foot on the battlefield (Bemidbar 14:1). Caleb and Yehoshua, the minority voice, desperately try to turn the crowd. They emphasize that the land is exactly as promised and that victory is guaranteed, with G-d leading the way. They describe the enemy as lahmenu, literally "our bread" or "our sustenance." They weren't just saying victory was possible; they were saying that just as we consume bread easily, we will consume the enemy completely with ease (Bemidbar 14:6-9).

What is the meaning behind these contrasting symbols, grasshopper versus bread? Why was there such a massive disparity between the two groups? The ten spies focused entirely on the physical size and strength of the inhabitants. Yet looking deeper, the real problem wasn't what they saw out there, but rather it was what they saw when they looked inward. The Torah records their exact words, "we were like grasshoppers in our own eyes, and so we were in their eyes." They didn't just report on the enemy's strength; they revealed a total collapse of self-worth (Sforno, Bemidbar 13:33). Once they viewed themselves as insignificant, that insecurity became their reality.

On the other hand, Caleb and Yehoshua used the imagery of bread to make a point. Bread is the staple of life, our daily sustenance, and synonymous with our parnassah (livelihood). In the desert, the Jewish People received their bread, the mann, as a direct daily gift from G-d. This was precisely the message Caleb and Yehoshua were sending. Yes, these enemies appear formidable, but just as we employ our faith and trust in G-d to provide us with our bread every single day without fail, He too will provide our victory. The enemies, however formidable, are no match for us as long as we keep our faith and trust in G-d.

In the end, G-d determines whether we win or lose, but that doesn't mean our game plan changes; we still have to play our part. Our Rabbis teach us that we must do our absolute best, and G-d will do the rest (Shabbat 104a). Even when a task seems impossible, when your back is against the wall and the seconds are ticking down, G-d can tip the odds in your favor in an instant, enabling you to score the winning shot against all possibilities. Don't be like the ten spies who let negativity consume them. In the blink of an eye, our entire trajectory can change. Have faith in G-d, and no matter what the scoreboard says, you will always come out on top.
